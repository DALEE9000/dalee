Ten persons in a room are wearing badges marked 1 through 10. Three persons are chosen at random, and asked to leave the room simultaneously. Their badge number is noted.

  1. What is the probability that the smallest badge number is 5?

    Permutation approach. There are 10 ⁣P3=10!7!{}^{10}\!P_{3} = \frac{10!}{7!}10P3​=7!10!​ ways to arrange a trio from a group of ten. Now, there are six badges numbered 5,...,10, inclusive. Thus, there are 6 ⁣P3=6!3!{}^{6}\!P_{3} = \frac{6!}{3!}6P3​=3!6!​ ways to arrange a trio involving the six highest numbers. The aim is to now determine how many of those trios actually contain 5. Since 5 is the lowest number in choosing only from this group, if the person with badge 5 is in the trio, then 5 will necessarily be the lowest number. Then the next step is to count the number of trios that do not contain badge 5; namely, the total arrangements of trios from the numbers 6, ..., 10. There are five such numbers, meaning there are 5 ⁣P3=5!2!{}^{5}\!P_{3} = \frac{5!}{2!}5P3​=2!5!​ such trios. Less the total arrangements from the trios excluding 5 from the total arrangements of trios from the six highest numbers and divide by the total number of trios from the group of ten to calculate: (6 ⁣P3−5 ⁣P3)/10 ⁣P3=1/12({}^{6}\!P_{3} - {}^{5}\!P_{3}) / {}^{10}\!P_{3} = \boxed{1/12}(6P3​−5P3​)/10P3​=1/12​.

    Combination approach. There are (103)=10!3!7!\binom {10}{3} = \frac{10!}{3!7!}(310​)=3!7!10!​ ways to choose 3 from a group of 10. Since there are five people with badges greater than 5, if 5 is fixed as the lowest badge number, then there are (52)=5!2!3!\binom 52 = \frac{5!}{2! 3!}(25​)=2!3!5!​ ways of choosing the remaining two people of those five. Ergo, there is a (52)/(103)=1/12\binom{5}{2} / \binom {10}{3} = \boxed{1/12}(25​)/(310​)=1/12​ probability that the smallest badge number is 5.

  2. What is the probability that the largest badge number is 5?

    Permutation approach. As before, there are 10 ⁣P3=10!7!{}^{10}\!P_{3} = \frac{10!}{7!}10P3​=7!10!​ ways to arrange a trio from a group of ten. Instead of six badges as previously considered, now there are only five badges in the group numbered 1, ..., 5, inclusive. From this group, there are 5 ⁣P3=5!2!{}^{5}\!P_{3} = \frac{5!}{2!}5P3​=2!5!​ arrangements. Now we must less this number of arrangements with the number of arrangements not including badge 5 in the trio, i.e., the trios formed from the group 1,..., 4. This latter arrangement totals simply to 4 ⁣P3=4!1!{}^{4}\!P_{3} = \frac{4!}{1!}4P3​=1!4!​. Then the probability is calculated by (5 ⁣P3−4 ⁣P3)/10 ⁣P3=1/20({}^{5}\!P_{3} - {}^{4}\!P_{3}) / {}^{10}\!P_{3} = \boxed{1/20}(5P3​−4P3​)/10P3​=1/20​.

    Combination approach. Now, there are four people with a badge number less than 5. Thus in fixing 5 as the largest badge number of the trio, there are (42)=4!2!2!\binom 42 = \frac{4!}{2!2!}(24​)=2!2!4!​ ways of choosing the remaining two people. Proceeding as in part (a), there is a (42)/(103)=1/20\binom{4}{2} / \binom {10}{3} = \boxed{1/20}(24​)/(310​)=1/20​ probability that the largest badge number is 5.

    In both (a) and (b), the combination approach proved simpler.
