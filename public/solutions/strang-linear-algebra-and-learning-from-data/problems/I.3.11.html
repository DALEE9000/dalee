(i) Dimension equals 0, 1, or 2. Intuitively, consider S and T completely orthogonal to one another. Then dim⁡ ⁣(S∩T)=0\dim \!\left( \textbf{S} \cap \textbf{T} \right) = 0dim(S∩T)=0. Alternatively, if S and T have one “overlapping" dimension, dim⁡ ⁣(S∩T)=1\dim \!\left( \textbf{S} \cap \textbf{T} \right) = 1dim(S∩T)=1. Analogously for two overlapping dimensions.

(ii) Dimension equals 7, 8, or 9. Imagine two independent vectors in three-dimensional space summed. The very act of summing takes us from the one dimension of the first vector to two dimensions. We may motivate this logic to say that adding vectors that are independent of those in our subspace increases the dimension of the sum of subspaces. Similarly, we appeal to the intuition of overlapping dimensions. If S is itself a subspace of T (i.e. S completely overlaps with T), then dim⁡ ⁣(S+T)=7\dim \!\left( \textbf{S} + \textbf{T} \right) = 7dim(S+T)=7. In the alternative, if we have only one overlapping dimension, then the dimension of the subspace sum goes up to 8, and two overlapping dimensions leads to 9.
